Loss     Definition
For insurance purposes, the Estimated Maximum  Loss (EML) is defined as:
'The loss that could be sustained under abnormal conditions with the failure of all protective systems.'
No credit is given to the effectiveness of barriers or the impact of emergency response activities.
The Estimated Maximum   Loss scenario is considered a credible event. While probability is not quantified, it is
appreciated by its nature that the Estimated Maximum Loss scenario has a very low likelihood of occurring.
The Estimated Maximum   Loss reflects our opinion of the ‘ground up’ exposure to Underwriters based on the policy in
place at the time that the survey was conducted. It does not necessarily represent the total exposure to the Client,
and may  be capped in a number of areas by policy limits.

Marsh has  developed its own in-house loss estimating software, Marsh BLAST powered by          (Marsh
BLAST),  to calculate Property Damage losses for Vapour Cloud Explosion (VCE) scenarios. Developed in
collaboration with Baker Engineering and Risk Consultants Inc. (BakerRisk), Marsh BLAST incorporates fundamental
research in explosion technology, and utilises the well-established Baker-Strehlow-Tang (BST) explosion model.
The application of the BST method in Marsh BLAST incorporates the following features:
•   A stoichiometric, hemispherical shaped vapour cloud created from the adiabatic flash of a liquid source
    inventory, or the jet momentum mixing of a gaseous source.
•   The potential for a cloud generated from a liquid source to drift until the Lower Flammable Limit (LFL) is reached,
    while the centre of a cloud generated from a gaseous source remains within the cloud radius of the leak location.
•   An explosion (potentially multiple, simultaneous blasts) occurring in areas of confined or semi-confined plant.
•   Explosion flame speeds based  upon fuel reactivity, and the congestion and confinement of structures.
•   A pressure / impulse distance profile established from the BST curves.
•   Damage   contours based upon overpressure, with the appropriate overpressure being a function of the
    explosion’s overall flame speed.
•   Blast and fire damage differentiated by structure type.

Recommendations    are raised towards the elimination or mitigation of major accident hazards, primarily based on
incidents arising from a loss of containment of material, whether this arises from natural, location, operation, or
design hazards.
The ISO  31000 standard defines five processes of risk management. Four of these processes form a cycle and the
fifth is an overarching activity. The cycle comprises:
•  Establishing the risk context.
•  Risk assessment.
•  Risk treatment.
•  Monitoring and review.
The overarching process connected  with all of these is communication and consultation. Marsh’s risk
recommendation   process is designed to assist our clients with all five of these processes.
A likelihood and consequence matrix is used to carry out the risk assessment. Following the ISO 31010 standard this
approach  is considered strongly applicable to risk identification and risk analysis with respect to consequence,
probability, and level of risk.
Each  recommendation  is evaluated and classified with respect to:
•  Consequence   Class (People, Environment, Reputation and Financial Loss) and Consequence  Severity (1 - 5): A
   Consequence   Class is selected for the incident(s) or loss(es) under consideration.
•  Likelihood (A - E): A Likelihood is selected for the failure of the identified barrier.
Both of these are as defined in the following tables, although it should be noted that the likelihood is not considered to
be the likelihood of the specified consequence, but is a likelihood of the failure of the identified barrier, which
recognises there are many similar barriers protecting a number of similar incidents. A Category of Risk is then
assigned to this as the final table in this section, which in turn can be used to provide a priority within the Client’s own
systems.

The ISO  31000 standard defines five processes of risk management. Four of these processes form a cycle and the

•  Establishing the risk context.
•  Risk assessment.
•  Risk treatment.
•  Monitoring and review.
The overarching process connected  with all of these is communication and consultation. Marsh’s risk
recommendation   process is designed to assist our clients with all five of these processes.

approach  is considered strongly applicable to risk identification and risk analysis with respect to consequence,
probability, and level of risk.

Recommendations    are raised towards the elimination or mitigation of major accident hazards, primarily based on
incidents arising from a loss of containment of material, whether this arises from natural, location, operation, or
design hazards.
The ISO  31000 standard defines five processes of risk management. Four of these processes form a cycle and the
fifth is an overarching activity. The cycle comprises:
•  Establishing the risk context.
•  Risk assessment.
•  Risk treatment.
•  Monitoring and review.
The overarching process connected  with all of these is communication and consultation. Marsh’s risk
recommendation   process is designed to assist our clients with all five of these processes.
A likelihood and consequence matrix is used to carry out the risk assessment. Following the ISO 31010 standard this
approach  is considered strongly applicable to risk identification and risk analysis with respect to consequence,
probability, and level of risk.
Each  recommendation  is evaluated and classified with respect to:
•  Consequence   Class (People, Environment, Reputation and Financial Loss) and Consequence  Severity (1 - 5): A
   Consequence   Class is selected for the incident(s) or loss(es) under consideration.
•  Likelihood (A - E): A Likelihood is selected for the failure of the identified barrier.
Both of these are as defined in the following tables, although it should be noted that the likelihood is not considered to
be the likelihood of the specified consequence, but is a likelihood of the failure of the identified barrier, which
recognises there are many similar barriers protecting a number of similar incidents. A Category of Risk is then
assigned to this as the final table in this section, which in turn can be used to provide a priority within the Client’s own
systems.

If insurable damage has resulted to a process unit, the costs of repair or rebuilding will be a claim against the

If a process unit has incurred substantial damage and is not operational for a long time it is likely that the following

•  An initial period of assessment and implementation of actions to be taken.
•  A minimum  operational activity period.
•  A build-up of business to projected levels of operation.
If for example a catastrophic loss has occurred to a substantial amount of the plant, where one or more process units

Recommendations    are raised towards the elimination or mitigation of major accident hazards, primarily based on
incidents arising from a loss of containment of material, whether this arises from natural, location, operation, or
design hazards.
The ISO  31000 standard defines five processes of risk management. Four of these processes form a cycle and the
fifth is an overarching activity. The cycle comprises:
•  Establishing the risk context.
•  Risk assessment.
•  Risk treatment.
•  Monitoring and review.
The overarching process connected  with all of these is communication and consultation. Marsh’s risk
recommendation   process is designed to assist our clients with all five of these processes.
A likelihood and consequence matrix is used to carry out the risk assessment. Following the ISO 31010 standard this
approach  is considered strongly applicable to risk identification and risk analysis with respect to consequence,
probability, and level of risk.
Each  recommendation  is evaluated and classified with respect to:
•  Consequence   Class (People, Environment, Reputation and Financial Loss) and Consequence  Severity (1 - 5): A
   Consequence   Class is selected for the incident(s) or loss(es) under consideration.
•  Likelihood (A - E): A Likelihood is selected for the failure of the identified barrier.
Both of these are as defined in the following tables, although it should be noted that the likelihood is not considered to
be the likelihood of the specified consequence, but is a likelihood of the failure of the identified barrier, which
recognises there are many similar barriers protecting a number of similar incidents. A Category of Risk is then
assigned to this as the final table in this section, which in turn can be used to provide a priority within the Client’s own

Loss     Definition
For insurance purposes, the Estimated Maximum  Loss (EML) is defined as:
'The loss that could be sustained under abnormal conditions with the failure of all protective systems.'
No credit is given to the effectiveness of barriers or the impact of emergency response activities.
The Estimated Maximum   Loss scenario is considered a credible event. While probability is not quantified, it is
appreciated by its nature that the Estimated Maximum Loss scenario has a very low likelihood of occurring.
The Estimated Maximum   Loss reflects our opinion of the ‘ground up’ exposure to Underwriters based on the policy in
place at the time that the survey was conducted. It does not necessarily represent the total exposure to the Client,
and may  be capped in a number of areas by policy limits.

Marsh has  developed its own in-house loss estimating software, Marsh BLAST powered by          (Marsh
BLAST),  to calculate Property Damage losses for Vapour Cloud Explosion (VCE) scenarios. Developed in
collaboration with Baker Engineering and Risk Consultants Inc. (BakerRisk), Marsh BLAST incorporates fundamental
research in explosion technology, and utilises the well-established Baker-Strehlow-Tang (BST) explosion model.
The application of the BST method in Marsh BLAST incorporates the following features:
•   A stoichiometric, hemispherical shaped vapour cloud created from the adiabatic flash of a liquid source
    inventory, or the jet momentum mixing of a gaseous source.
•   The potential for a cloud generated from a liquid source to drift until the Lower Flammable Limit (LFL) is reached,
    while the centre of a cloud generated from a gaseous source remains within the cloud radius of the leak location.
•   An explosion (potentially multiple, simultaneous blasts) occurring in areas of confined or semi-confined plant.
•   Explosion flame speeds based  upon fuel reactivity, and the congestion and confinement of structures.
•   A pressure / impulse distance profile established from the BST curves.
•   Damage   contours based upon overpressure, with the appropriate overpressure being a function of the
    explosion’s overall flame speed.
•   Blast and fire damage differentiated by structure type.
•   Model  calibrated to reflect industry loss experience.

Marsh has  developed its own in-house loss estimating software, Marsh BLAST powered by          (Marsh
BLAST),  to calculate Property Damage losses for Vapour Cloud Explosion (VCE) scenarios. Developed in

The application of the BST method in Marsh BLAST incorporates the following features:
•   A stoichiometric, hemispherical shaped vapour cloud created from the adiabatic flash of a liquid source
    inventory, or the jet momentum mixing of a gaseous source.
•   The potential for a cloud generated from a liquid source to drift until the Lower Flammable Limit (LFL) is reached,
    while the centre of a cloud generated from a gaseous source remains within the cloud radius of the leak location.
•   An explosion (potentially multiple, simultaneous blasts) occurring in areas of confined or semi-confined plant.
•   Explosion flame speeds based  upon fuel reactivity, and the congestion and confinement of structures.
•   A pressure / impulse distance profile established from the BST curves.
•   Damage   contours based upon overpressure, with the appropriate overpressure being a function of the
    explosion’s overall flame speed.
•   Blast and fire damage differentiated by structure type.
•   Model  calibrated to reflect industry loss experience.

The ISO  31000 standard defines five processes of risk management. Four of these processes form a cycle and the
fifth is an overarching activity. The cycle comprises:
•  Establishing the risk context.
•  Risk assessment.
•  Risk treatment.
•  Monitoring and review.
The overarching process connected  with all of these is communication and consultation. Marsh’s risk
recommendation   process is designed to assist our clients with all five of these processes.
A likelihood and consequence matrix is used to carry out the risk assessment. Following the ISO 31010 standard this
approach  is considered strongly applicable to risk identification and risk analysis with respect to consequence,
probability, and level of risk.
Each  recommendation  is evaluated and classified with respect to:
•  Consequence   Class (People, Environment, Reputation and Financial Loss) and Consequence  Severity (1 - 5): A
   Consequence   Class is selected for the incident(s) or loss(es) under consideration.
•  Likelihood (A - E): A Likelihood is selected for the failure of the identified barrier.
Both of these are as defined in the following tables, although it should be noted that the likelihood is not considered to
be the likelihood of the specified consequence, but is a likelihood of the failure of the identified barrier, which
recognises there are many similar barriers protecting a number of similar incidents. A Category of Risk is then
assigned to this as the final table in this section, which in turn can be used to provide a priority within the Client’s own

Loss     Definition
For insurance purposes, the Estimated Maximum  Loss (EML) is defined as:
'The loss that could be sustained under abnormal conditions with the failure of all protective systems.'
No credit is given to the effectiveness of barriers or the impact of emergency response activities.
The Estimated Maximum   Loss scenario is considered a credible event. While probability is not quantified, it is
appreciated by its nature that the Estimated Maximum Loss scenario has a very low likelihood of occurring.
The Estimated Maximum   Loss reflects our opinion of the ‘ground up’ exposure to Underwriters based on the policy in
place at the time that the survey was conducted. It does not necessarily represent the total exposure to the Client,
and may  be capped in a number of areas by policy limits.

Marsh has  developed its own in-house loss estimating software, Marsh BLAST powered by          (Marsh
BLAST),  to calculate Property Damage losses for Vapour Cloud Explosion (VCE) scenarios. Developed in
collaboration with Baker Engineering and Risk Consultants Inc. (BakerRisk), Marsh BLAST incorporates fundamental
research in explosion technology, and utilises the well-established Baker-Strehlow-Tang (BST) explosion model.
The application of the BST method in Marsh BLAST incorporates the following features:
•   A stoichiometric, hemispherical shaped vapour cloud created from the adiabatic flash of a liquid source
    inventory, or the jet momentum mixing of a gaseous source.
•   The potential for a cloud generated from a liquid source to drift until the Lower Flammable Limit (LFL) is reached,
    while the centre of a cloud generated from a gaseous source remains within the cloud radius of the leak location.
•   An explosion (potentially multiple, simultaneous blasts) occurring in areas of confined or semi-confined plant.
•   Explosion flame speeds based  upon fuel reactivity, and the congestion and confinement of structures.
•   A pressure / impulse distance profile established from the BST curves.
•   Damage   contours based upon overpressure, with the appropriate overpressure being a function of the
    explosion’s overall flame speed.
•   Blast and fire damage differentiated by structure type.
•   Model  calibrated to reflect industry loss experience.

The particular curve to be selected to represent the relationships depends upon the flame speed within the vapour
cloud. In the BST model, the flame speed is dependent on the reactivity of the material within the vapour cloud, and
the congestion and confinement of the volume where the explosion occurs.
Therefore, Marsh BLAST  takes into consideration the potential escalation effects of plant congestion and
confinement as well as the material properties and hazardous material inventory.
Congestion refers to the obstacles that obstruct the passage of the flame front within in the developing VCE that
create turbulence and increase the flame speed without preventing expansion. Obstacle density can be classified as
Low, Medium  or High.
Confinement  refers to physical structures that obstruct expansion of the flame front within the developing VCE.
Confinement  can be classified as 3-D, 2.5-D or 2-D. A confinement classification of 3-D indicates that the flame front
is free to move in 3 dimensions (the ground notwithstanding). A classification of 2-D indicates that the flame front is
limited to movement in 2 dimensions (sandwiched between 2 planes e.g. a solid roof and the ground). The 2.5-D
classification is applied to situations which fall between 3-D and 2-D.
Fuel reactivity describes the propensity of a flame to accelerate in a developing VCE for a given fuel. Fuel reactivity
is classified as Low, Medium or High, depending on the laminar burning velocity and fuel expansion factor.
The Marsh  BLAST  software calculates an energy term, which is combined with the appropriate blast curve, to
determine the overpressure profile generated as a result of the VCE. The energy term is calculated for each location

Recommendations    are raised towards the elimination or mitigation of major accident hazards, primarily based on

The ISO  31000 standard defines five processes of risk management. Four of these processes form a cycle and the
fifth is an overarching activity. The cycle comprises:
•  Establishing the risk context.
•  Risk assessment.
•  Risk treatment.
•  Monitoring and review.
The overarching process connected  with all of these is communication and consultation. Marsh’s risk
recommendation   process is designed to assist our clients with all five of these processes.
A likelihood and consequence matrix is used to carry out the risk assessment. Following the ISO 31010 standard this
approach  is considered strongly applicable to risk identification and risk analysis with respect to consequence,
probability, and level of risk.
Each  recommendation  is evaluated and classified with respect to:
•  Consequence   Class (People, Environment, Reputation and Financial Loss) and Consequence  Severity (1 - 5): A
   Consequence   Class is selected for the incident(s) or loss(es) under consideration.
•  Likelihood (A - E): A Likelihood is selected for the failure of the identified barrier.

For insurance purposes, the Estimated Maximum  Loss (EML) is defined as:
'The loss that could be sustained under abnormal conditions with the failure of all protective systems.'
No credit is given to the effectiveness of barriers or the impact of emergency response activities.
The Estimated Maximum   Loss scenario is considered a credible event. While probability is not quantified, it is
appreciated by its nature that the Estimated Maximum Loss scenario has a very low likelihood of occurring.
The Estimated Maximum   Loss reflects our opinion of the ‘ground up’ exposure to Underwriters based on the policy in
place at the time that the survey was conducted. It does not necessarily represent the total exposure to the Client,
and may  be capped in a number of areas by policy limits.

Marsh has  developed its own in-house loss estimating software, Marsh BLAST powered by          (Marsh
BLAST),  to calculate Property Damage losses for Vapour Cloud Explosion (VCE) scenarios. Developed in
collaboration with Baker Engineering and Risk Consultants Inc. (BakerRisk), Marsh BLAST incorporates fundamental
research in explosion technology, and utilises the well-established Baker-Strehlow-Tang (BST) explosion model.
The application of the BST method in Marsh BLAST incorporates the following features:
•   A stoichiometric, hemispherical shaped vapour cloud created from the adiabatic flash of a liquid source
    inventory, or the jet momentum mixing of a gaseous source.
•   The potential for a cloud generated from a liquid source to drift until the Lower Flammable Limit (LFL) is reached,
    while the centre of a cloud generated from a gaseous source remains within the cloud radius of the leak location.
•   An explosion (potentially multiple, simultaneous blasts) occurring in areas of confined or semi-confined plant.
•   Explosion flame speeds based  upon fuel reactivity, and the congestion and confinement of structures.
•   A pressure / impulse distance profile established from the BST curves.
•   Damage   contours based upon overpressure, with the appropriate overpressure being a function of the
    explosion’s overall flame speed.
•   Blast and fire damage differentiated by structure type.
•   Model  calibrated to reflect industry loss experience.

The ISO  31000 standard defines five processes of risk management. Four of these processes form a cycle and the
fifth is an overarching activity. The cycle comprises:
•  Establishing the risk context.
•  Risk assessment.
•  Risk treatment.
•  Monitoring and review.
The overarching process connected  with all of these is communication and consultation. Marsh’s risk
recommendation   process is designed to assist our clients with all five of these processes.
A likelihood and consequence matrix is used to carry out the risk assessment. Following the ISO 31010 standard this
approach  is considered strongly applicable to risk identification and risk analysis with respect to consequence,
probability, and level of risk.
Each  recommendation  is evaluated and classified with respect to:
•  Consequence   Class (People, Environment, Reputation and Financial Loss) and Consequence  Severity (1 - 5): A
   Consequence   Class is selected for the incident(s) or loss(es) under consideration.
•  Likelihood (A - E): A Likelihood is selected for the failure of the identified barrier.
Both of these are as defined in the following tables, although it should be noted that the likelihood is not considered to
be the likelihood of the specified consequence, but is a likelihood of the failure of the identified barrier, which
recognises there are many similar barriers protecting a number of similar incidents. A Category of Risk is then
assigned to this as the final table in this section, which in turn can be used to provide a priority within the Client’s own
systems.
